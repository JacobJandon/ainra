What the root does

Four jobs. Done completely.

A root is trustworthy in proportion to how little it does. These four functions are held in common — everything else stays with registrars, operators, and verifiers. Even the examples on this page use placeholder registrars: the root treats everyone equally.

01 · Namespace

Names every agent

One canonical, resolvable name per agent — ainra:registrar:operator:lineage@version — that any system can look up and get the signed passport back.

BY DESIGN · NAMES POINT — REGISTRARS SERVE THE FILES.

02 · Accreditation

Licenses the passport offices

Independent registrars — licensed issuers, enterprise directories, permissionless chains — do the verifying and issuing. The root audits them against published rules and can revoke one wholesale.

BY DESIGN · REGISTRARS ISSUE — THE ROOT KEEPS THEM HONEST.

03 · Root of trust

Anchors one key hierarchy

Every passport chains up to one set of root keys, held in threshold by custodians across jurisdictions. A verifier anywhere checks a passport from anywhere, locally, in milliseconds.

BY DESIGN · THE ROOT HOLDS KEYS AND RULES — REGISTRARS HOLD THE RECORDS.

04 · Revocation & logs

Runs the switch and the record

One revocation feed every verifier subscribes to — a compromised version stops everywhere in under a minute — and every event is sealed in append-only, witness-cosigned logs.

BY DESIGN · THE ROOT RECORDS AND PROPAGATES — EVERY VERIFIER DECIDES.

The passport, field by field

Every line has a job.

Each numbered key below matches the marks ①–⑧ on the specimen passport at the top of this page.

1
LINEAGE

The agent's permanent identity — soulbound, held for life, the thing history attaches to. Is this the same agent I dealt with before?

2
VERSION

Re-certified on every material change — model, permissions, operator — so trust always describes what's running today. What exactly is running right now?

3
OPERATOR

The verified legal entity responsible for the agent — where accountability lives if something goes wrong. Who do I hold responsible?

4
REGISTRAR

The independent, accredited issuer that did the verification and signed the passport. Its certificate chains to the root. Who vouched, under what rules?

5
AUTHORITY CLASS

Who stands behind it: a human (proven in zero-knowledge), an organization, another agent along a provable chain — or, declared honestly, fully autonomous. On whose behalf is this acting?

6
ASSURANCE TIER

How much verification stands behind the passport, L0 to L4. The root labels; every verifier chooses its own floor. How much can this credential carry?

7
ISSUING AUTHORITY + MRZ

The machine-readable zone any system can parse in one pass — with the canonical name, validity dates, and key fingerprint printed above it. Mandates and running fleets live outside the document, at the edge, where they belong. Can my software read this without custom integration?

8
ROOT ATTESTATION

The live status: chain valid, witnesses agree, entry sealed in the public log, in good standing — re-checked at every verification. Is this still good, right now?

The technology, in plain words

Proven parts. New assembly.

Every piece is an open standard already trusted at internet scale. The new work is the assembly — and the charter that holds it neutral.

HTTP MESSAGE SIGNATURES

Every request an agent sends is cryptographically signed, and the signature names the key in its passport. The passport rides inside ordinary web traffic.

ROLE · HOW THE PASSPORT TRAVELS
SD-JWT VERIFIABLE CREDENTIALS

The passport's file format. Selective disclosure means the agent proves exactly the fields a counterparty needs — and keeps the rest private.

ROLE · THE DOCUMENT ITSELF
DECENTRALIZED IDENTIFIERS (DID)

A standard name format any system can resolve. Every ainra: name is a DID underneath, so existing tooling understands it out of the box.

ROLE · UNIVERSAL ADDRESSING
MTLS + SHORT-LIVED CREDENTIALS

Running copies carry minutes-long credentials minted locally under the passport, bound to the connection. A stolen token expires before it matters.

ROLE · THEFT CONTAINMENT AT SCALE
ZERO-KNOWLEDGE PROOFS

Prove "a verified human authorized this agent" while keeping the person private. Accountability and privacy, together.

ROLE · HUMAN BACKING WITH PRIVACY INTACT
CONSENT MANDATES

A signed permission slip from the principal — scopes, time limits, spend ceilings, a revocation address — that every downstream delegation can only narrow.

ROLE · AUTHORITY YOU CAN AUDIT AND RECALL
MERKLE TRANSPARENCY LOGS

Append-only records where altering history breaks the math, and independent witnesses cosign every checkpoint. Honesty is checkable, always.

ROLE · A RECORD THAT KEEPS ITSELF HONEST
THRESHOLD KEY CEREMONIES

The root key is split so signing takes a quorum of custodians across jurisdictions; used only in public, recorded ceremonies, with a standby in escrow.

ROLE · POWER SHARED BY CONSTRUCTION
STATUS PROOFS + PUSH REVOCATION

Compressed revocation sets update in seconds, and high-assurance interactions require a fresh proof — so the switch works even when someone blocks the feed.

ROLE · THE SWITCH, GUARANTEED
THE TRUST GRAPH

The log-derivable map of who acts for whom — delegation edges only, behavior stays private. Agents with expired mandates surface ecosystem-wide.

Assurance tiers

The lifecycle

Register. Present. Verify. Revoke.

Register

Through an independent registrar of your class. The lineage gets a soulbound passport, authority class declared, event sealed in the public log.

Present

The passport rides in the signed requests the agent already sends, disclosing exactly what each counterparty needs.

Verify

Anyone checks chain, authority, mandate, and live status against cached root keys — milliseconds, works offline.

Revoke

One logged event stops a version's entire fleet — or a principal recalls a mandate and its whole subtree — everywhere, in under a minute.

FAQ

The questions everyone asks.

Is AINRA a company?

It's an international, member-governed, non-profit federation — the organizational form that gave sport, aviation, and the internet their neutral coordinating bodies. Legitimacy flows from the members; the federation itself never competes with them. It holds no equity, charges capped cents-scale membership fees, and is charter-bound to stay out of issuing, scoring, payments, personal data — and out of favoring any registrar, even in its examples.

Who's behind this — and why should anyone trust you?

Deliberately, you shouldn't have to. The founding circle is forming now (that's what this page recruits), and the design assumes zero trust in the founders: the specification is public, the reference code is open-source and runnable in sixty seconds, the root keys will be split across custodians in five-plus jurisdictions, every action lands in witness-cosigned public logs — and the whole standard is forkable, so if we ever go wrong, the ecosystem leaves with everything and we keep nothing. Verify, don't trust, applies to us first.

Nobody's on it yet. Why would anyone join first?

Because the alternative is what everyone is doing now: wiring the same trust checks pairwise, over and over, at N² cost — every issuer integrating every verifier, separately, forever. A shared root turns that into one membership. And joining first isn't charity: verification is useful on day one with zero counterparties (the open CLI and test vectors stand alone), verifiers adopt to accept more of what already reaches them through one integration, and the founding table writes the rules everyone else will live under. The honest counter on this page — logs sealed: 0 — is the point: everything here is real, and the first seats are authorship, not sponsorship.

Who issues the passports, then?

Independent registrars — separate organizations accredited under identical published rules: licensed issuers, enterprise directories, permissionless chains. It works the way domain names do: you get your passport from a registrar of your choice, the registrar serves you, and every issuance is simultaneously a registration in the root registry — the authoritative record every registrar writes into. You can move a lineage between registrars with its history intact; the root itself sells nothing to anyone.

What if an agent has no human behind it?

Then its passport says so, honestly: authority class A4. Autonomous agents keep a reachable governance endpoint, renew on a dead-man timer, and post an accountability substitute — a bond, insurance, or a steward of record — to climb the tiers. Verifiers decide how much A4 they'll accept; existence itself is guaranteed.

Does it replace the registries and control planes that already exist?

It feeds them. Runtime engines keep making every decision; the root supplies the record each decision consumes. On-chain registries become permissionless registrars, enterprise directories mint passports at the boundary, and payment protocols keep their transport while their key lookups resolve through the root.

Who controls the root keys?

A quorum, always — threshold keys held by custodians across at least five jurisdictions and three institution types, rotated in public ceremonies, with a standby in escrow. Every action is sealed in witness-cosigned logs — and the whole root is forkable, so the standard outlives any institution.

How does this scale to billions of agents?

By hierarchy. The root talks to registrars. Registrars handle lineages and versions. Running copies use minutes-long credentials minted locally. Verification happens at the edge against cached keys. Add a trillion agents and the center's load stays flat.
